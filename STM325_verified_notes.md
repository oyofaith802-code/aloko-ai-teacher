## 1. BACTERIAL GROWTH CURVE

In a closed batch culture, a typical bacterial growth curve has four phases:

1. Lag phase: Cells adapt to the new environment, synthesise enzymes and cellular components, and prepare for division. Cell numbers may change little. Lag phase is not the stationary phase.
2. Log (exponential) phase: Cells divide at a near-constant maximum rate under the prevailing conditions. Population size increases exponentially.
3. Stationary phase: Nutrient limitation, oxygen limitation, waste accumulation, or other stresses restrict net growth. In the classical model, the rate of new cell formation approximately equals the rate of cell death, so viable counts remain roughly stable.
4. Death (decline) phase: Viable cell numbers decrease because cell death exceeds new cell formation. Some organisms may persist or form resistant structures.

The duration of each phase depends on the organism, medium, temperature, pH, oxygen availability, and other conditions. Do not give a universal doubling time.

## 2. CELLULAR RESPIRATION AND FERMENTATION

Respiration transfers electrons through an electron transport chain (ETC) to a terminal electron acceptor. The resulting energy gradient can drive ATP synthesis by oxidative phosphorylation.

### Aerobic respiration
- Oxygen (O2) is the terminal electron acceptor.
- An electron transport chain is used.
- ATP can be produced by substrate-level phosphorylation and oxidative phosphorylation.
- Complete oxidation of glucose commonly yields much more ATP than fermentation, but the yield varies by organism and biochemical system.
- The 36–38 ATP figure is a traditional textbook estimate, not a universal measured yield. Avoid presenting it as exact.

### Anaerobic respiration
- Uses an electron transport chain but uses a terminal electron acceptor other than oxygen.
- Examples of acceptors in particular microorganisms include nitrate (NO3-), sulfate (SO4^2-), and fumarate.
- The acceptor depends on the organism; not every microorganism can use every acceptor.
- ATP yield varies with the electron donor, acceptor, organism, and pathway. Do not assign a universal 2–4 ATP yield.
- Anaerobic respiration is not synonymous with fermentation.

### Fermentation
- Does not use an electron transport chain for ATP generation.
- Electrons from organic substrates are transferred to an organic molecule derived from the substrate, regenerating NAD+ so glycolysis or another fermentation pathway can continue.
- ATP is produced mainly by substrate-level phosphorylation.
- Common examples include lactic acid fermentation and alcoholic fermentation.
- In common homolactic fermentation and alcoholic fermentation pathways starting from one glucose molecule, the net yield is typically 2 ATP per glucose.
- Fermentation products vary with the organism and pathway.

### Essential comparison
- Aerobic respiration: ETC present; oxygen is the terminal acceptor.
- Anaerobic respiration: ETC present; a non-oxygen terminal acceptor is used.
- Fermentation: no respiratory ETC; an organic molecule accepts electrons; ATP is generated mainly by substrate-level phosphorylation.

## 3. GLYCOLYSIS

- Glycolysis converts one glucose molecule into two pyruvate molecules.
- Its net yield per glucose is 2 ATP and 2 NADH.
- Glycolysis does not directly require oxygen.
- The fate of pyruvate depends on the organism and environmental conditions.
- Fermentation can regenerate NAD+ by transferring electrons to organic intermediates.

## 4. CITRIC ACID CYCLE

- The citric acid cycle (TCA/Krebs cycle) oxidises acetyl-CoA and generates reduced electron carriers.
- Per acetyl-CoA, the usual accounting is 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, and 1 GTP or ATP, with 2 CO2 released.
- One glucose commonly gives two acetyl-CoA, so the cycle turns twice per glucose when the relevant pathway is operating.
- Some microorganisms use modified or incomplete versions of the cycle. Do not assume every microbe has an identical pathway.

## 5. ELECTRON TRANSPORT AND ATP

- NADH and FADH2 carry electrons to an electron transport chain in organisms that use that chain.
- Electron transfer helps establish an electrochemical gradient.
- ATP synthase uses this gradient to produce ATP by oxidative phosphorylation.
- Do not claim that all microorganisms have mitochondria. Bacteria perform respiratory electron transport across their cytoplasmic membrane.
- ATP yield depends on the organism, transport costs, pathway, coupling efficiency, and terminal electron acceptor.
- Avoid treating traditional fixed ATP totals as universal facts.

## 6. MICROBIAL GROWTH FACTORS

Microbial growth is affected by nutrient availability, temperature, pH, water availability, oxygen requirements, osmotic conditions, and inhibitory substances. The optimum varies by species.
